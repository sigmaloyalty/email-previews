December is the season of giving. There are gifts for the people we love and more online orders in a few weeks than most of us place all year. The lights are up, the cocoa is hot, and the shopping carts are full.


Scammers have noticed too. This year they haven’t dreamed up many new tricks. They’ve just gotten very good at running a store. Familiar holiday scams are faster to build, more polished, and harder to tell from the real thing. The good news is that a few updated habits will keep you a step ahead.

The Grinch Has Opened a Department Store


Just a few years ago, shopping scams were crude, single-product cons — one too-good-to-be-true item, a blurry photo, and a payment button. Today, scammers fake the whole store. Reports are that a new fake store pops up roughly every three minutes, and these stores come with full catalogues, return policies, and real ad campaigns behind them. Many visits to them start with an ad on a major platform such as Meta or Google.


The lesson is clear: a store can look professional, have glowing reviews, and be gone before your gift arrives, which is why “it looks legitimate” and “it has reviews” no longer tell you much on their own.


Check the Workshop Before You Check Out


Before You Buy:


  • Find the store yourself. If an ad or social post catches your eye, open a new tab and search for the retailer directly rather than clicking through.
  • Read the web address carefully. Watch for extra words like “sale,” “outlet,” or “store,” added hyphens, or unfamiliar endings. The padlock means your connection is encrypted. It doesn’t mean the seller is trustworthy.
  • Find out who’s behind it. Look for a real business name, an address, and working contact details. Then search the store’s name along with “scam” or “complaint.”
  • Compare the price. Sales are everywhere in December, which makes impossible discounts easier to hide in the crowd. If a deal is far below what established retailers charge, walk away.
  • Ignore the countdown clock. A real sale will still be there after you’ve checked.


When You Pay:


  • Use a credit card. Credit cards generally give you a way to dispute a charge. Be wary of any seller who asks for an Interac e-Transfer, gift cards, or cryptocurrency.


When Your AI Shopping Elf Gets Fooled Too


More of us are asking AI assistants to compare prices, suggest gifts, and sometimes even complete the purchase. It can be a real time-saver. But these helpers are not seller inspectors.


The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security’s holiday shopping guidance says AI tools “cannot reliably determine whether a seller, website or offer is trustworthy or fraudulent.” Its advice is to stay actively involved and treat an AI recommendation as a suggestion, not a guarantee. In other words, an assistant that found the gift and the lowest price hasn’t necessarily confirmed that the store is real.


If you shop with an AI assistant, keep the final say:


  • Require your approval before any purchase.
  • Set clear limits, such as a spending cap and a list of retailers you already trust.
  • Remove saved payment details it doesn’t need.
  • Open the actual seller’s site yourself before you check out.


The Mystery Gift That Wants You to Scan


Here’s a twist on a scam you may already know. An unordered package shows up with your name on it, usually holding something cheap like seeds, wipes, or a phone cable. It’s called “brushing.” A seller uses your name and address to post fake “verified purchase” reviews.


The 2026 version adds a small card inside that says something like “Scan to see who sent your gift” or “Scan to start your return.” Consumer protection agencies have warned that the code can lead to a phishing website built to steal your passwords or card details.


If a package you didn’t order arrives:


  • Don’t scan anything inside it, and don’t call any number printed on it.
  • Report it to the marketplace through its official app or website.
  • Treat it as a reminder that your name and address are circulating. Update your shopping account passwords and turn on multi-factor authentication wherever it’s offered.


The Gift Card Switcheroo


We’ve shared this one before, and it’s back because it keeps working. Scammers tamper with gift cards on store racks, either swapping barcodes or recording card numbers and PINs. Once you buy and load the card, they drain the balance, often before the gift is even unwrapped. The recipient is left with an empty piece of plastic.


A few quick habits help:


  • Choose cards kept behind the counter, or buy e-gift cards directly from the retailer’s own website.
  • Check that the packaging is intact, the PIN area is untouched, and the barcode lies flat with no raised edges.
  • Keep your receipt until the recipient has used the card, and encourage them to use it soon.
  • Remember that no legitimate seller, agency, or “support” agent will ever ask you to pay with gift cards.

When Holiday Shopping Goes Wrong

Even careful shoppers get caught. If something doesn’t feel right after you’ve paid:


  1. Don’t panic, but act quickly. Contact your credit card company or bank right away.
  2. Keep the evidence. Save screenshots, receipts, and order confirmations before the site disappears.
  3. Secure your accounts. Change passwords on any account that may be affected, and on any that share the same password.
  4. Report it. File a report through Canada’s Report Cybercrime and Fraud system at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca, even if you didn’t lose money. Each report helps others.
  5. Get expert help. If your personal information was compromised, Securizone’s identity restoration specialists are here to guide you through every step.
