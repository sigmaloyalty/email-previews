November is Financial Literacy Month in Canada, and this year’s theme is once again “Talk Money.” It’s a simple idea with a big payoff: the more comfortable we get talking about money, the more confident we feel making decisions about it.


Financial literacy isn’t about memorizing terms. It’s about being ready when real decisions show up, like taking out a mortgage, getting a handle on debt, choosing between financial products, or spotting a scam before it costs you. Those moments go better when we’ve already had the conversation.


There’s one conversation we tend to skip: fraud. According to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre, Canadians lost over $704 million to fraud in 2025. And that figure only tells part of the story, because the CAFC estimates that just 5% to 10% of frauds are reported.


Why Fraud Is So Hard to Talk About


Fraud carries a stigma that most financial missteps don’t, and that stigma works in fraudsters’ favour.


Anyone can be targeted. Fraudsters don’t check your age, education, income, or background first. They build convincing messages, calls, and websites designed to work on smart, careful people. Being targeted says nothing about your intelligence.


Silence helps the wrong people. When people feel embarrassed, they keep quiet. That means the warning signs never reach the friends and family who could have used them. Every story that’s shared makes the next scam a little easier to spot.

Money Moments Worth Talking About


You don’t need to be an expert, just open to asking questions and sharing what you know. Here are four moments where a conversation can make a real difference:


  1. Big purchases and borrowing. Whether it’s a mortgage, a car loan, or a major purchase, talk it through with someone you trust before you commit. A second set of eyes helps you ask better questions, and it makes a too-good-to-be-true offer easier to spot.
  2. Managing debt. Debt is one of the hardest money topics to raise, which is exactly why it helps to raise it early. Be cautious of anyone who promises a quick fix or asks for payment upfront.
  3. Choosing financial products. Compare your options, ask what you don’t understand, and take your time. Pressure to decide right now is a signal to slow down.
  4. Fraud and near-misses. Share the suspicious texts, calls, and emails you’ve received, especially the ones you didn’t fall for. Near-misses are some of the most useful stories to tell.


Making the Conversation Easier


Talking about money doesn’t have to feel awkward. A few ideas to help:


Start small and specific. Instead of a broad money talk, begin with a real situation: “I got a strange text about a package today. Have you seen anything like it?”


Go first. Sharing your own experience, including the times you were caught off guard, makes it easier for others to open up.


Keep it judgment-free. The goal is to look out for each other, not to evaluate each other’s decisions.


Make it a habit. Regular check-ins feel more natural than crisis conversations and keep the topic from feeling heavy.


The Canadian Anti-Scam Coalition’s Stand Against Scams campaign sums up a good response to anything suspicious in three words: Stop. Check. Talk. That last step is the heart of Financial Literacy Month.


When Something Feels Off, Here’s What to Do


Even with great conversations behind you, a suspicious message will eventually land in your inbox. Knowing what you’ll do before it happens makes it much easier to stay calm. Here’s a simple routine worth sharing with the people you care about:


  1. Pause. Scam messages are designed to rush you with urgent deadlines, threats, or too-good-to-be-true offers. Taking a moment before you click, reply, or pay takes away their biggest advantage.
  2. Verify independently. Don’t use the links or contact details in the message itself. Instead, contact the organization directly using details you already have, like those on your card, your statement, or the official website.
  3. Talk to someone. Show the message to a family member, friend, or colleague and ask what they think. A second opinion is often all it takes to spot what you missed, and it’s another chance to turn a near-miss into a shared lesson.
  4. Report it. If you or someone you know has been targeted by fraud, contact your local police and report it to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre through the Report Cybercrime and Fraud website. Report it even if no money was lost. Every report helps investigators connect the dots and spot new tactics sooner.


It also helps to keep this routine somewhere easy to find, such as a note on the fridge or a message pinned in your family group chat. If everyone in your circle knows the steps, nobody has to figure them out under pressure.

This Financial Literacy Month, Start the Conversation

Talking about money won’t make fraud disappear, but it does make us harder to fool, quicker to ask for help, and more willing to speak up when something goes wrong. That’s worth far more than the awkward first minute.


This November, pick one money conversation to have with someone you care about. It could be with a parent, a partner, a friend, or a young person who’s managing money for the first time. Start small, and see where it goes.


Remember to stay vigilant, stay informed, and stay safe.
