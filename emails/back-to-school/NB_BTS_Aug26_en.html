August involves something easy to overlook in the back-to-school rush: an unusual volume of personal information changing hands all at once. Rental applications. Tuition payments. Financial aid forms. Part-time job onboarding. New campus accounts. All of it legitimate, all of it necessary – and all of it exactly what fraudsters have been waiting for.


Identity theft doesn’t always require a dramatic breach or a sophisticated hack. Sometimes it only requires the right email arriving at the right moment. Fraudsters map the back-to-school calendar deliberately, timing their scams around moments when sharing personal information feels normal, even expected.


There are three pressure points this season that are worth knowing about before they arrive in your inbox.

Before You Move In: The Application That’s Actually a Data Grab


The paperwork comes before the packing boxes – and some of it isn’t what it appears to be.


Fraudulent rental listings near campus follow a predictable pattern: well-photographed, priced just below the area’s going rate, and accompanied by a “streamlined application” asking for a SIN, banking information, an employment letter, photo ID, and references. The landlord is conveniently out of the country and needs everything sent by email before any viewing can be arranged. Once that package is submitted, it doesn’t matter whether the apartment was ever real. The applicant has provided enough to open credit accounts, file a fraudulent tax return, or take out a loan in their name. International students are particularly exposed, since many need to confirm housing before they’ve arrived in Canada.


The same pattern shows up in job offers. A message on LinkedIn or Indeed promises flexible remote work, and within hours an “onboarding package” arrives: SIN for T4 purposes, a void cheque for direct deposit, a driver’s licence scan for work eligibility, sometimes a passport photo for “international payroll.” A legitimate employer collects this documentation only after a contract is signed, through a secure system – never in the first exchange after a brief conversation online.


The signal in both cases is the same: sensitive identity documents, requested before any face-to-face contact, through an informal channel, under time pressure. Real paperwork doesn’t work that way. If an application asks for a SIN before you’ve met anyone in person, that’s the moment to stop.


First Week on Campus: The Email From Your School That Isn’t


The first two weeks of the academic year bring a flood of legitimate administrative email – portal setup, password resets, new multi-factor authentication rollouts, orientation details, financial aid confirmations. Students are conditioned to expect it and act on it quickly. Fraudsters count on exactly that.


Phishing emails designed to look like messages from IT services, the registrar, financial aid offices, or even a department chair ask recipients to verify or reactivate their credentials through a linked page. The stakes go beyond a compromised password: a single stolen login can unlock academic records, direct deposit details, financial aid documents, and the student’s primary email account – which then becomes the key to resetting passwords on every other service.


Parents see a variation of the same attack: messages that name the student specifically, claiming a financial aid form needs an urgent signature or that a tuition payment “failed” and requires reauthorization through a new portal.


The tell is always in the same place. No legitimate institutional email will ask you to log in through a link leading outside the school’s official domain. When something prompts a login, go directly to the portal through your browser rather than following the link. That habit – just that one – closes off almost every version of this scam.


Once You’re Settled: The Call No Parent Is Ready For


With students moved in and the semester underway, a different kind of scam turns its attention to the people waiting at home.


The call is always urgent. There’s been an accident, an arrest, a situation that needs money wired immediately and quietly, before anyone else finds out. The voice on the line sounds exactly like the student – because voice-cloning technology can now replicate someone’s voice from a small sample of audio scraped from public social media.


The Canadian Anti-Scam Coalition’s guidance applies directly here: Stop. Check. Talk. When urgency is being used against you, slowing down is the most powerful thing you can do.


The most effective protection can be arranged before move-in day: agree on a family code word with your student. Something only the two of you know, kept off social media entirely, required before any emergency request is taken at face value. If a panicked call arrives and the caller can’t produce it, hang up and call your student back at the number you have for them.


Any request that combines urgency with secrecy – “please don’t tell anyone, I’m embarrassed” – isn’t a sign the situation is real. It’s the scam’s design. Police, lawyers, and hospitals will never ask for payment by gift card, cryptocurrency, or wire transfer.

When the Worst Happens, You’re Not Alone


What makes identity theft more damaging than a typical fraud is its timeline. A stolen SIN may not surface in a fraudulent credit application for months. A compromised student email can quietly forward messages to a fraudster for weeks before anyone notices. Information sold on the dark web gets used on someone else’s schedule, not yours – and by the time the consequences appear, tracing them back to August can feel nearly impossible.
